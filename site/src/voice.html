How it learns your voice

Three to five samples. One distillation. Then it is yours to edit.

A voice is not a tone setting. It is the words you reach for, how long your sentences run and how much that varies, how you open and close, the small moves that give you away and how often you actually make them. That is what the profile records, with quotes from your samples as evidence.

Paste what you actually sent

A couple of emails, a few chat messages, something longer if you have it. Type or paste each one into the Voice tab, or drop a file. Tag where it came from. Three to five is plenty to start.

samples stay in extension storage

Distill it once

The model reads the samples and writes the profile under six headings: diction, sentence rhythm, structural habits, tics and fingerprints with their frequency, baseline intensity, and what you would never do. It is plain text. Read it, fix it, or add a one-line correction that every run follows and every later distill keeps. Samples stay editable, and the widget says when the profile has fallen behind them.

one call · versioned · editable

Write, in any register

Paste a draft and pick Rewrite, Tighten, or Warmer; or pick Draft and say what the message should do. The profile and the register’s rules ride along on every run. Copy the result, or send it back in and go again.

### Diction & vocabulary Short words, plain verbs. “Honestly” opens a sentence about once per message, never twice. Contractions throughout; “folks” for a group; never “utilize”, never “leverage”. ### Sentence rhythm A long sentence, then a short one. Fragments when the point is already made (“Both mine. Both easy.”). Semicolons, rarely; parentheses, never. ### Tics & fingerprints One exclamation point per email at most, usually none. Signs off with a first name and no “Best”. Hedges with “I think” about once per paragraph, then commits. ### What this author would never do Open with “Hey everyone!”. A bullet list in a chat message. A closing paragraph that restates the email.

The method, samples distilled once into a profile that rides along on every run, comes from Dickens, the voice engine by the same author. Fogar carries a light version of it.

Registers

One voice. Several registers. Rules that are followed to the letter.

You do not write emails the way you write chat messages, and neither is a different person. A register is the same voice at a different formality and length, with rules of its own. Three come built in. Add the rest: a LinkedIn post, release notes, texts to your mother.

Email

Professional but warm. Concise; a few short paragraphs, no padding. Opens with the point, not throat-clearing; signs off naturally.

built in

Chat

Casual, conversational. Terse; one to a few lines, often a single thought. Lowercase is fine, fragments are fine, no sign-off, no bullet lists.

built in

Essay

Considered, literary. Flowing long-form; paragraphs breathe and arguments develop. Vary the rhythm hard; specific detail over generality.

built in

Yours

A name, a formality, a length, and rules. “No em dashes” is more than a hint: after the model answers, any em dash it slipped in is turned into a comma, so the rule holds even when the model forgets.

Straight talk

A portrait, not a costume.

The rest of Fogar is a new tab that answers from a model running in your browser. See what leaves in each mode.

  • The profile is only as good as the samples. Five things you actually sent beat twenty you polished for the occasion.
  • On this device, the 4B tier distills a usable profile and rewrites in it well; the smaller tiers produce vaguer profiles. The model is a menu in the widget’s header, not a commitment: distill with a cloud model you trust and run locally after, or the other way round.
  • It aims for you on an ordinary day, at your natural intensity, with your tics at their real rate. It will not turn one exclamation point into five.
  • It is not a humanizer and it does not try to beat AI detectors. It writes in your voice because it has your voice; that is the whole method.
  • The small models read about a thousand words at a time. Distilling uses the checked samples that fit, and you choose which by checking them; a run takes about two pages of text. Cloud models take far more.
Questions

Fair questions, short answers.

How many samples does it need?

Three to five pieces of your real writing, across a couple of registers, produce a profile worth using. More helps, up to about a dozen. Paste what you actually sent, not what you wish you had sent; the profile is a portrait of you on an ordinary day.

Where do my samples go?

Into Chrome’s extension storage on this machine, and into the backup file if you download one. On this device they are read by the model running in your browser and never leave it. If you pick a cloud model in the widget’s header, the samples go to that endpoint once, when you press Distill, and the profile and your text go there on each run. Nothing passes through Fogar.

Can I read or change the profile?

Yes. It is plain text under six headings. Open the Voice tab, press Edit, fix what the model got wrong or add a rule of your own, and save. Every run uses the profile as you left it. Distilling again writes a new version.

What is a register?

The same voice at a different formality and length, with rules of its own. Email, Chat, and Essay come built in, and you can add any number more. Rules are followed exactly, and “no em dashes” is enforced after the model answers as well, so the rule holds even when the model forgets.

Can I keep more than one voice?

Yes. One Writing widget is one voice. Add a second widget from the Add widget menu, name it after the client or the brand, and feed it their samples. The two never mix.

Is this a humanizer?

No. It does not try to defeat AI detectors, and it is not for passing off someone else’s text as your own. It rewrites and drafts in your voice, from your samples, and the result reads as you because it is built from you.

Does it work offline?

Yes, in local mode, once the model is cached. Samples, profile, and registers are on your disk; turn off Wi-Fi and it keeps writing.
